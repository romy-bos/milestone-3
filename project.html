The Campus Game: What’s Really Causing Concussions?

Soccer is one of the most popular sports on college campuses, but head injuries remain a serious hazard. When people imagine a sport-related concussion (SRC), they usually picture two players crashing together mid-air or someone violently striking the turf.

A comprehensive study from the NCAA-DoD CARE Consortium (Jo et al., 2024, published in The American Journal of Sports Medicine) analyzed 391 soccer concussions across collegiate programs to uncover how players actually get hurt—and how long it takes them to get back on the pitch.

ImportantSurprising Finding

Heading the ball isn’t just routine soccer play—it is the #1 single cause of collegiate soccer concussions, accounting for nearly one in three injuries overall and over 51% of concussions sustained during team practice.

Part 1: How Do Concussions Happen?

The study classified every concussion into four main injury mechanisms:

  1. Head-to-Ball: Direct contact with the ball (e.g., contested headers, high-speed deflections, or close-range shots).
  2. Head-to-Body: Striking another player’s shoulder, elbow, or torso during a challenge.
  3. Head-to-Head: Direct skull-to-skull collisions with an opponent or teammate.
  4. Head-to-Ground / Equipment: Hitting the turf, goalposts, or boundary barriers.

Part 2: The Road to Recovery — How Long Are Athletes Sidelined?

How long does it take for a student-athlete to be fully cleared for unrestricted return-to-play (URTP)?

Athletes don’t simply jump back into games right after feeling better. They must complete a supervised graduated return-to-play protocol, progressing from light aerobic exercise to sport-specific drills, non-contact practice, full practice, and finally game clearance.

NoteWhy Did Ball Impacts Lead to Longer Recoveries?

Athletes who hit the ground or equipment recovered quickest (averaging 14.0 days), while head-to-ball impacts kept athletes out the longest (averaging 18.6 days). Researchers note that repetitive headers during training sessions can cause cumulative sub-concussive micro-trauma before a diagnosed concussion occurs, potentially slowing neurocognitive recovery.

Part 3: Practice vs. Game Day Reality

The danger profile changes drastically depending on whether athletes are in drills or playing a competitive match:

Environment Top Danger Why It Happens
Practice Head-to-Ball (51.1%) Repetitive heading drills, crosses, and close-quarter scrimmage shots.
Competition Player Collisions (66.8%) Full-throttle aerial challenges and contested loose balls (head-to-body: 36.0%, head-to-head: 30.8%).

Takeaways for College Athletes & Coaches

  • Smarter Training: Limit high-repetition heading drills in training to curb cumulative head impacts.
  • Fair Play Enforcement: Strict referee enforcement on reckless challenges in the air can reduce high-speed head-to-head collisions during match play.
  • Listen to Your Body: Because ball-impact concussions can take nearly three full weeks to heal, athletes should never rush back through concussion protocol before fully clearing clinical benchmarks.

Source: Jo, J., Boltz, A. J., Williams, K. L., et al. (2024). Mechanisms of Injury Leading to Concussions in Collegiate Soccer Players: A CARE Consortium Study. The American Journal of Sports Medicine, 52(6), 1585–1595.
